Released in 1972, **Pong** became one of the best-known early arcade video games. Its design was deliberately straightforward: two paddles moved up and down, and players batted a square ball back and forth across the screen. The first player to miss gave the other a point.

Pong was developed by Atari, the company founded by **Nolan Bushnell** and **Ted Dabney**. Bushnell asked engineer **Allan Alcorn** to build a practice project based on electronic table tennis. Alcorn refined the idea with features such as sound effects and a score display. Atari installed an early machine at Andy Capp’s Tavern in Sunnyvale, California, in 1972. It proved so popular that the machine reportedly stopped working after its coin box filled up.

Pong arrived during a period when video games were still an emerging novelty. Its recognizable sports theme made the technology easy to understand for people who had never played a video game before. Atari soon manufactured arcade cabinets for bars, restaurants, and other public venues.

The game also moved into homes. In 1975, Atari released a home version through Sears under the name **Tele-Games Pong**. It connected to a television and used dedicated controllers, bringing the arcade-style experience into living rooms.

Pong was not the first electronic table-tennis game: **Magnavox Odyssey’s Table Tennis** had appeared earlier in 1972. The similarities led to a legal dispute between Magnavox and Atari, which Atari settled. Even so, Pong became an important commercial breakthrough and helped establish the public’s appetite for video games. Its paddles and bouncing ball remain a concise example of how much play can come from a few simple rules.
