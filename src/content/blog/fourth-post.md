**Tetris** is built around a simple task: arrange falling geometric pieces to complete horizontal lines. When a line is filled, it disappears and frees space. The game ends when the pieces stack to the top. This easy-to-grasp idea has supported decades of increasingly fast, satisfying play.

The game was created in 1984 by **Alexey Pajitnov**, a computer engineer working at the Dorodnicyn Computing Centre in Moscow. He drew inspiration from pentomino puzzles, in which shapes made from five squares are fitted together. For the computer version, he used pieces made from four squares; the name Tetris combines “tetra,” meaning four, with “tennis,” one of Pajitnov’s favorite sports.

Pajitnov first programmed the game for the **Elektronika 60**, a Soviet computer with a text-based display. The earliest version did not have the colorful blocks most players know today, but the central challenge was already there. The game spread among colleagues and then to other computers, becoming popular across the Soviet Union and beyond.

Releasing Tetris internationally proved complicated because the rights passed through several organizations during the late 1980s. Different companies negotiated rights for computer, arcade, and console versions. This period of negotiations became part of the game's wider history as it moved from a lab project into a worldwide commercial success.

A major turning point came in 1989, when Nintendo released Tetris for the **Game Boy**. The portable format suited the game’s short rounds, and its inclusion in many Game Boy bundles helped it reach a huge audience. Players could carry it anywhere, while the handheld’s link cable also allowed two systems to compete.

Tetris has since appeared on nearly every generation of gaming hardware, with new modes and visual styles layered over its enduring core. Its story is a reminder that a game does not need a complicated premise to last: a few clear rules, meaningful decisions, and the urge to try one more round can be enough.
